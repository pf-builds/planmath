On a family plan, who pays what?

A family plan stacks a second layer on top of the usual deductible and out-of-pocket max. Put in a year of expected care for each person and watch where the money actually lands, which caps get hit, and in what month. Then flip the plan structure and watch the same year cost something different.

This is one of the densest topics in health insurance, and this page shows a lot at once. On a phone, load a scenario below and scroll through the results. On a wider screen you can see the whole year in one view.

Every term on this page, in plain language
Deductible
The amount you pay yourself each year before your plan starts sharing the cost of care.
Coinsurance
Your fixed percentage share of a bill after the deductible is met. Here you pay 20%, the plan pays 80%. It's automatic, not a fee you choose.
Copay
A flat fee for a specific service (like $50 for a prescription), separate from the deductible.
Out-of-pocket max
Your yearly ceiling. Once your payments add up to this, the plan pays 100% for the rest of the year.
Preventive care
Routine care like checkups and screenings, covered 100% before the deductible applies.
Subscriber and dependents
The subscriber is the person the plan is issued to, usually through their job. Everyone else covered under it is a dependent, which can mean a spouse or partner, a child, or anyone else the plan allows. On this page they're all just people with their own expected care.
Family deductible
The most your household pays in deductible across everyone combined. Once the family total reaches it, nobody pays deductible for the rest of the year, even a member who never met their own.
Family out-of-pocket max
Your household's yearly ceiling across everyone. Once combined payments reach it, the plan pays 100% for every member for the rest of the year.
Embedded plan
Each person has their own individual deductible and out-of-pocket max, and the family caps sit on top. A single high-cost member stops at their individual cap, and the family cap catches the aggregate.
Aggregate (non-embedded) plan
There are no individual caps. The whole family deductible has to be met, in any combination across members, before coinsurance starts for anyone, and the family out-of-pocket max is the only ceiling. Common on high-deductible plans.
The one that trips people up

Coinsurance is not a second insurance. It's your share of one bill. On a $1,000 bill after your deductible, at 20% coinsurance:

$200 · you $800 · plan

Once your deductible's met, every bill splits this way until you reach your out-of-pocket max.

How this is calculated

Expenses are processed in month order, because that's how a real plan year works. Each standard expense runs the usual path: you pay dollar-for-dollar until your deductible is met, then coinsurance on the rest, until your out-of-pocket max stops you and the plan covers 100%. Copays are a flat fee that skips the deductible but still counts toward the out-of-pocket max. Preventive care is covered in full and counts toward nothing.

The difference between the two plan structures

On an embedded plan, two limits apply to every bill at once: the member's own remaining deductible, and the family's remaining deductible, whichever is smaller. The same is true of the out-of-pocket max. That's why a single sick member on an embedded plan stops at the individual amount, no matter how large their bills get. On an aggregate plan there is no individual limit in the way, so one member can meet the entire family deductible alone and keep paying right up to the family out-of-pocket max. Same family, same care, different bill.

What changes when someone else joins your plan

Everything above still applies to you personally, and a second set of caps appears on top: a family deductible and a family out-of-pocket max that everyone's payments count toward together. The consequence people miss is that you can stop paying a deductible because of someone else's bills, without ever meeting your own. Switch to family mode above to see that happen, and to compare the two ways plans handle it.

Why the month on each expense matters

For one person the yearly total usually lands in the same place no matter what order the bills arrive in. What the month changes is when your costs stop. If you're going to hit your out-of-pocket max in August, everything after that is covered in full, which is worth knowing before you decide whether to schedule something this year or wait until January when the whole thing resets to zero.

Say the family deductible is met in May. Anyone who gets care in June skips the deductible entirely and goes straight to coinsurance, even if they'd never had a claim all year. Move that same care to March and they'd have paid a deductible first. That timing effect is invisible if you just add up the year's spending, which is exactly why this calculator walks the year in order instead.

A note on copays and high-deductible plans

This calculator applies copays the same way under both structures, so that flipping the toggle changes one thing and one thing only, the plan structure. Worth knowing in real life: aggregate deductibles usually appear on high-deductible health plans, and those generally can't offer flat copays before the deductible is met, apart from preventive care. On a real HDHP your prescriptions would most likely run through the deductible instead.

What this doesn't account for

Premiums aren't included, since they're a flat monthly cost that doesn't interact with any of this. If you're weighing premium against out-of-pocket exposure, the plan cost comparison calculator handles that side. This also assumes every expense is in network, that the plan uses a single deductible rather than a separate one for prescriptions, and that every dollar you pay counts toward the out-of-pocket max. Check your plan's summary of benefits for which of those hold.
